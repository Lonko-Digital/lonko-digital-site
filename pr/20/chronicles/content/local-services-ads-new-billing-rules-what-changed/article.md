Every advertising platform you use has a quiet rulebook for what you're actually paying for. A "click." A "lead." A "conversion." Most days that rulebook sits in the background and you never think about it. Then the platform rewrites a line in it, and your bill changes before you've decided anything.

That's exactly what just happened to Local Services Ads.

If you run Local Services Ads and a bill recently included a charge for a call you never answered, you didn't miss anything. Google changed the rule on purpose, and it's part of a bigger shift in how the whole program works.

## What happened

Google is doing two things to Local Services Ads at once. First, it's retiring the standalone LSA dashboard and moving campaigns into Google Ads itself, as a new kind of Performance Max campaign built around paying per lead instead of per click. The first group of advertisers, mostly US home and storefront businesses like plumbing, HVAC, electrical, and pest control, started migrating in August 2026. A second group, including service-area businesses without a storefront, is scheduled for late 2026, with everyone else following into 2027.

Second, and separately, Google changed what counts as a billable call lead. As of October 1, 2026, a missed call during your posted business hours becomes a charged lead if the caller stays on the line for more than 20 seconds, even though you never picked up. Google says the goal is to reward businesses that respond quickly and reliably, since people searching for a plumber or an electrician want to reach someone fast.

## Why it matters

Local Services Ads is pay-per-lead, not pay-per-click, so the definition of "lead" is the entire basis of what you're charged for. Changing that definition changes your bill, and it changes it retroactively from the customer's perspective: the same call that used to cost you nothing because nobody answered can now cost you something because the caller waited.

This isn't a vague platform tweak. It has a specific, checkable mechanism, and that's actually good news, because it means you can verify exactly how it applies to your business rather than guessing.

## How the new billing rule actually works

A missed call is only charged if the caller stays connected more than 20 seconds during your business hours, as set in your Google Business Profile. If your ad schedule is different from your posted hours, a missed call only gets charged when it falls inside both.

If your phone system routes to an automated menu, the 20-second clock doesn't start until the caller presses a key, and a caller who hangs up without pressing anything isn't charged at all. A call that reaches voicemail or a recorded greeting without a key press, though, is still treated as connected, and can be charged the same way a missed call can.

Every lead now shows a clear status in your account: Charged, Not charged, In review, or Credited. Google says it keeps assessing lead quality for 30 days after a lead comes in, and automatically credits ones it decides were poor quality, duplicate, or invalid. If the same customer calls or messages again within 15 days, you're only billed once for that relationship, not once per call. Google's own Ads Liaison, Ginny Marvin, confirmed advertisers "will only be charged 1x" for that kind of follow-up contact.

One limit worth knowing: automatic lead credits aren't available for health care or tax-specialist advertisers, or for businesses in the EU/EEA.

## What else is changing with the migration

Separate from billing, the move into Google Ads changes some operational details. Weekly budgets become daily averages. Manual bidding and a per-vertical Target CPA go away, replaced by one campaign-level Target CPA, so a business running multiple service categories loses the ability to bid them differently inside a single campaign. BBB callouts are discontinued, so you'll want at least a handful of other structured callouts ready to take their place. Your business name, address, and hours now sync one-way from your Google Business Profile, and a significant change to any of those can trigger a short verification review, sometimes pausing your campaign for a day or two.

What doesn't change: you still pay per valid lead rather than per click, your ads still show up only on Google Search and Maps, and targeting is still keywordless, based on your service categories and areas. Your lead history, including contact details and past messages, carries over, and so does your Google Verified badge.

## What to actually do

If you haven't migrated yet, download or screenshot your historical performance reports now. Once an account migrates, old campaign-level and ad-level performance data doesn't carry over into the new reporting, even though your lead history does. Google sends a notice 14 days before your migration date and a reminder 7 days before, so check that your account administrator's email is current and that someone is actually opted in to lead notifications.

If you've already migrated, look at your Leads page and get familiar with the Charged/Not charged/In review/Credited statuses before you need them to make sense of a confusing bill. If a lead looks wrong, Google's feedback survey on that lead is the way to flag it for review.

## What not to overreact to

This isn't Google trying to quietly inflate your bill. The quality-assessment and credit system is still there, the 15-day repeat-contact rule stops you from being charged twice for the same customer, and the IVR exceptions mean a caller who hangs up on your phone tree doesn't cost you anything. If your team is genuinely answering calls or picking up messages promptly, most of this change shouldn't move your spend much. It mainly matters for calls that currently go unanswered longer than 20 seconds, which is worth knowing even apart from the billing change.

## What to watch next

The second migration phase, covering service-area businesses without a storefront, lands in late 2026, with non-US accounts following in 2027. Until Google opens up creating new pay-per-lead campaigns directly inside Google Ads, new LSA campaigns still have to start at g.co/localservices. If your business is scheduled for a later phase, treat the next few months as a preview of what's coming rather than something to act on today, and keep an eye on your migration email so you're not caught by the 14-day notice window.

## The bigger lesson here

Local Services Ads isn't the only place this can happen. Any platform you advertise on, Google Ads, Meta, LinkedIn, even your email platform, can quietly redefine what counts as a lead, a click, a subscriber, or a conversion, and that definition is what your bill and your results are actually measuring. The fix isn't to distrust every platform update. It's to build the habit of reading the actual definition whenever one changes, the way you just did with this one, so a billing or reporting change never catches you off guard again.
